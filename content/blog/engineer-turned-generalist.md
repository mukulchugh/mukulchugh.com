For a long time I measured myself by how well I wrote code. Cleaner abstractions, faster ship times, fewer bugs. That's a fine thing to be good at, and getting good at it took years — mobile at Swiggy, scaling a platform at Zenduty until it was acquired, a lot of nights.

Then I became a founding engineer at an AI startup, and the ground shifted under the thing I'd optimized for.

## Code got cheaper, judgment got expensive

The uncomfortable truth of building in 2026 is that writing code is no longer the bottleneck. AI writes a lot of it now, and it writes the boilerplate faster than I can. The [Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/being-a-founding-engineer-at-an-ai) and basically everyone hiring founding engineers say the same thing: you're [no longer evaluated on how fast you type](https://www.coffeespace.com/blog-post/what-startups-look-for-in-founding-engineers-in-2026), but on how well you leverage AI to build the right thing.

Which means the scarce skill isn't production — it's **deciding what to produce**. What to build, what to cut, what "done" means for this feature this week. That's judgment, and no model has your context.

## The specialist silos quietly closed

When I was purely an engineer, there was always a wall I could stand behind: "that's a product decision," "that's a design call," "that's the backend team." At a small startup those walls don't exist, and I've come to think they were never as load-bearing as they felt.

The strongest founding engineers today [pair full-stack skill with product sense](https://www.secondtalent.com/resources/how-to-become-founding-engineer-startup/) — they talk to customers, they connect the dots between what a user is stuck on and what the code should do, and they ship the whole thing. Companies now actively [avoid engineers who only worked on isolated components](https://grizmolabs.com/how-to-hire-the-right-founding-engineer-for-your-startup-in-2026/). The generalist isn't a compromise anymore. It's the default.

## What "generalist" actually means (and doesn't)

I want to be careful here, because "generalist" can sound like "mediocre at everything." That's not it.

- It's **not** giving up depth. My mobile and full-stack foundation is what lets me move fast across the stack. You earn the right to be a generalist by being good at something first.
- It **is** refusing to hide behind a role. Design, product, support, debugging someone else's mess — you cross into all of it to get to the truth of the problem.
- It **is** being AI-native. Not "I used a chatbot once," but treating AI as a real part of how you build and validate — [now table stakes for the role](https://futurense.com/blog/ai-skills-in-demand).

The mental shift is from "what's my specialty" to "what does this problem need, and can I go get it."

## What I'd tell my earlier self

Three things.

1. **Ship the whole thing, not the demo path.** The last 5% — the edge cases, the empty states, the error you'll never hit in the demo — is where product quality actually lives, and it's the part that's tempting to skip when you're moving fast.
2. **Get close to the user.** The best feature ideas I've had came from watching someone struggle, not from a spec. Engineering that never touches the user is just guessing with extra steps.
3. **Stay curious across the whole stack.** The interesting problems live in the seams between disciplines — where product meets engineering meets design. That's where a generalist gets to do their best work.

I still love writing code. I just stopped thinking it was the job. The job is making something people want, shipping it, and making it actually work — and code is one of the tools, not the point.
